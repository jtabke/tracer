## 1. Establish whose work we are designing for

**Confirmed discovery focus:** use medical-device engineering as a familiar reference while comparing documentation-heavy medical-device/aerospace and lighter software/technology workflows from the outset. Preserve configurable terminology, relationship meanings and completeness expectations without forcing a fixed hierarchy or approval process. Formality varies within industries. This is not an exclusive market decision, permission to implement workflow configuration, or a compliance claim.

**User-reported primary journeys:** (1) build a new design by authoring successive requirements/design layers and establishing coverage; (2) sustain an existing product by proposing a change and understanding the consequences of modifying or removing existing information. These are different work contexts, not a decision to create two new pages. Actual task artifacts and independent user observations are still needed.

Candidate working roles to investigate, not invented personas:

- Author: structures and revises information, often in batches.
- Connector: establishes and checks relationships across levels or disciplines.
- Reviewer: understands a proposal, its evidence and changes before making a decision.

One person may perform all three. Distinguish frequent daily work from occasional review, first-time project setup, and schema administration. Do not give all these activities equal prominence by default.

Use formal/lightweight variants of the agreed task to challenge domain and process assumptions from the outset. Do not attempt to optimize for every industry or organizational role in the first round.

### Gather actual work, not preferences about screens

Ask users to walk through a recent task using their existing spreadsheet, document, requirements tool or review package. With permission, use sanitized artifacts; never require confidential medical or research information.

Capture:

- What triggered the work, the starting information and the desired finished result.
- Typical steps, handoffs, collaborators, interruptions and return-to-work behavior.
- What users look at together, what they edit repeatedly, and what they avoid changing accidentally.
- Their language for items, parents, siblings, links, coverage, evidence and review.
- Common scale and awkward cases: shared children, multiple parents, missing links, long text, inconsistent fields, dense connections.
- Time lost, uncertainty, workarounds and the consequences of mistakes.

Do not ask which of our current tabs they prefer. Ask them to demonstrate what they need to accomplish.

**Output:** a small set of observed task stories with artifacts and evidence. Mark each finding as observed, inferred or still hypothetical.

For sustaining, test whether users can explore a proposed change before committing it, understand the relationship paths that make an item potentially affected, and distinguish removal from temporary filtering. Preview/staging of proposals is a design hypothesis, not an implemented engine capability. ### Quantitative requirements: progressive structure, not compulsory formalization

The product owner raised a new hypothesis: important numbers/specifications/limits in requirements, including template-assisted EARS-style statements, may need explicit meaning and dependency tracking rather than remaining undifferentiated text. Investigate this inside both journeys; do not turn it into a mandatory requirements language or an automatic interpretation engine.

Explore three optional levels:

1. **Ordinary text:** a user can write and review a requirement without structured parameters. Template assistance may help wording but must remain optional.
2. **Named values and constraints:** users can explicitly identify a value, unit, comparator, range or tolerance and reference it in prose. Distinguish a shared parameter from two coincidentally equal numbers. Preserve the meaning and value the author/reviewer actually saw.
3. **Declared quantitative dependencies:** users may explain that a limit is inherited, allocated, derived by an explicit expression, or related only by engineering judgment. Calculation is optional and allowed only where the meaning, units and relation are explicitly established.

A parent/child relationship alone must not imply arithmetic propagation. A shared value reference, an allocation/budget, a formula and an independent design choice are different situations. Direction matters: changing a parent limit may require reconsidering child allocations; changing a child may affect parent feasibility without changing the parent requirement itself.

On a proposed quantitative change, explore presenting the old/new value, declared consumers, dependency paths and the reason each item needs attention. Distinguish:

- Directly changed information.
- A computable result or violated constraint under an explicitly modeled rule.
- Potential engineering impact requiring human interpretation.
- Unknown/unmodeled dependencies; absence of a declaration is not proof of no impact.

Never silently rewrite child requirements, reallocate budgets, revise test acceptance criteria or mark evidence applicable. Offer explainable proposals, with human review and ordinary guarded writes. Optional extraction from prose, if explored later, should produce suggestions for confirmation—not authoritative dependencies inferred from every number.

If an allocation example is useful, the optional toy timing budget is a 100 ms overall limit and 60 ms/40 ms allocations; it is not the selected task or a requirement that all mockups model budgets. Only calculate their sum if the user explicitly models that combination, including relevant overhead assumptions. Changing 100 ms to 80 ms should expose the allocation question, not automatically scale both children. These are illustrative numbers, not medical-device specifications.

Test free-text-only, mixed structured/text, inconsistent units, ranges/tolerances, a shared value used in several places, an unrelated identical number, and removal of a referenced parameter. Start with one real quantitative example before adding a formula/rules editor. Leave storage representation open; reuse existing typed fields/entities where sufficient rather than assuming a new primitive is required.

## 3. Preserve outcomes, challenge UI assumptions

### Non-negotiable outcomes

- Same underlying identity and data across representations.
- Applicable actions available in context; no artificial workflow barrier because the user chose a particular representation.
- Clear scope: which items, relationships, versions and evidence are being shown.
- Shared children are not copies; a network is not forced into a tree.
- Coverage, evidence freshness and review decisions remain distinguishable.
- Drafts, conflict handling, unknown states and read-only history cannot mislead or silently lose work.
- Keyboard and accessible interaction are first-class; narrow layouts retain orientation.

### Open design questions

- Task-first versus project-structure-first entry.
- Separate pages versus a stable working set with switchable representations.
- Document, spreadsheet or relationship-chain authoring.
- Inline editing versus a contextual panel versus a dedicated focused editor.
- How to move between a branch, siblings, a single item and the larger network.
- How to expose advanced detail without overwhelming routine work.
- How users recognize a view's scope and recover when a result is too dense.

**Operation parity does not mean identical controls everywhere.** A representation can put its common actions directly on the canvas or cells and expose less frequent actions through a consistent contextual workspace. Test whether the complete task is possible without needless navigation, not whether every toolbar contains every button.

## 5. Evaluate on real tasks

Run a small formative round with approximately 4–6 representative participants where feasible, including an occasional user/reviewer and a frequent author. This is qualitative discovery, not statistically representative proof. If only the product owner is available, label that a provisional walkthrough rather than validated usability.

First compare concept comprehension and task approach using sketches. Narrow to two candidates, then test their core interactions with comparable fidelity. Counterbalance presentation order; use equivalent task variants so learning the first design does not make the second look better.

Candidate task script, to be replaced/refined with observed work:

1. Find the relevant need or requirement and explain its scope.
2. Trace it through downstream information to an output; identify a missing connection.
3. Inspect a child, find its siblings and recognize its other parent without mistaking it for a duplicate.
4. Make a realistic field or batch change and connect the intended existing item.
5. Explain a changed input and make an appropriately scoped review decision.
6. Leave and resume the task; handle a stale version or failed read without losing the proposal.

Introduce increasing complexity only after the basic journey works: longer text, heterogeneous fields, duplicate names, shared children and dense branches. The existing 1k/10k synthetic fixtures are engineering stress cases, not a substitute for representative task data.

### Assess more than speed or preference

Record completion and errors, moderator assistance, hesitation, unnecessary switches, loss of scope/context, confidence and ability to explain the result. Measure time only after the tasks are understood; fewer clicks alone is not success.

Check especially whether participants:

- Know what is selected, shown, hidden and editable.
- Can find the next action without being taught our UI structure.
- Understand the relationship direction and sibling group.
- Distinguish a coverage link from evidence applicability.
- Recover safely from mistakes and interruptions.

Record findings by task and severity, with observation evidence. Keep accessibility checks separate from a general visual preference score. No regulatory approval or technical validity follows from a successful usability task.

## 6. Selection gate before implementation

Choose a concept, or revise and retest, based on observed task fit—not visual polish, votes or reuse of current code. Agree on task-specific success criteria before the sessions; avoid inventing numerical success claims from a tiny sample.

Do not advance with unresolved safety/comprehension failures: misleading scope, silent draft loss, accidental edits, identity confusion or treating connected evidence as automatically valid. Repeated core-task failures or substantial coaching require another iteration.

A selected design needs:

- Defined first users, primary task and explicit non-goals.
- Evidence explaining why its structure fits the work better than alternatives.
- A navigation/context model and an operation map by working level.
- Complete designs for empty, large, interrupted, conflicting and historical states.
- An accessible keyboard path through the primary task.
- Known limitations and an honest list of remaining assumptions.

Only then implement one complete, thin journey against the existing engine. Test it with users again before adding more representations or broad feature coverage. Preserve or remove prototype code based on usefulness, not sunk cost.
